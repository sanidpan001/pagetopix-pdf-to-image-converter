Why convert PDF pages to images at all?

PDF is the right format for documents and the wrong format for almost everything else. Slides want images. Social posts want images. Web forms that demand 'upload a photo of the document' want images, and design tools place images far more gracefully than embedded PDF pages. Converting a PDF page to PNG or JPG is one of those small, constant chores of digital life — signing a scanned agreement, lifting a chart from a report, turning an invoice into a thumbnail — and it should take seconds, not a software hunt. PageToPix does it where you are, in the tab you already have open.

The catch with most online converters is not quality but custody. The typical workflow uploads your document — which might be a contract, a medical report or a payslip — to a stranger's server, converts it there, and asks you to trust a deletion promise. PageToPix inverts the model: a rendering engine runs inside your browser, the file is read from your disk into your tab's memory, and the images are generated and downloaded locally. No upload exists, so no retention policy matters.

How browser-side PDF rendering works

Your PDF is rendered by pdf.js, the open-source engine originally built by Mozilla that also powers Firefox's built-in PDF viewer. It interprets the same page descriptions — text, vector shapes, embedded images and fonts — and paints them onto an HTML canvas at the resolution you choose. That canvas is then exported as a PNG or JPG file. Because the engine is the very code that displays PDFs for hundreds of millions of people, fidelity is high: what you saw in your viewer is what lands in the image.

Resolution is the one decision that matters. At 150 DPI a page is perfectly legible on screens and roughly a megapixel or two in size. At 300 DPI — the print standard — fine print survives enlargement and the file grows accordingly. PageToPix lets you pick per job rather than accepting a converter's hidden default, and it renders page by page so that a 200-page document does not try to hold 200 print-resolution canvases in memory at once.

PNG or JPG: choosing the output format

Choose PNG when the page is mostly text, line art or interface elements: PNG's lossless compression keeps edges razor sharp and is the safe choice for anything that will be read closely, archived or edited further. Choose JPG when the page is photographic — a scanned photo document, a glossy brochure — because JPG's lossy compression produces far smaller files for continuous-tone content, and at the quality PageToPix uses, the difference is invisible in practice.

One JPG subtlety is handled for you conceptually: JPG has no transparency, so transparent regions flatten onto white — the standard behaviour readers expect from a document page. If you plan to composite a page over a coloured background, PNG preserves your options. When in doubt, export PNG; storage is cheap and sharpness is forever.

Quality tips for scans and small text

If your source PDF is itself a scanned image, the output can never be sharper than the scan inside it — raising the export to 300 DPI enlarges the pixels faithfully but invents no detail. For crisp results, start from PDFs produced digitally (exported from Word, a browser or a design tool) whenever you have the choice. Small text benefits most from PNG at 200–300 DPI; dense financial tables stay readable roughly one generation longer than at 150.

Check one page before downloading fifty: render, zoom to 100 percent, and read the smallest text on the page. If it is clean at your chosen setting, the rest of the document will behave the same way. That ten-second check, plus choosing the format deliberately, is the whole craft of PDF-to-image conversion done well.

Working with very long documents

For a several-hundred-page PDF, resist converting everything 'just in case'. Render the document, scan the preview grid, and download only the pages with a destination — the deck, the form, the post. Each unneeded page is a file to name, store and later delete, and batches of two hundred images have a way of becoming permanent clutter. If you truly need the whole set, download-all works page by page precisely so big jobs stay stable; afterwards, rename files immediately with the document name plus page number, the pattern the downloader already starts for you. Future searches will thank present discipline: 'annual-report-page-014' is findable forever, while 'IMG-final-final2' is a small tragedy in every archive it haunts.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my PDF uploaded anywhere?

No. The file is read into your browser tab and rendered locally by the pdf.js engine. Nothing is transmitted to a server, so there is nothing to store, leak or delete.

What resolution should I choose?

150 DPI is plenty for screens and sharing. Choose 300 DPI for print or when small text must stay crisp when enlarged. Higher resolution means larger files and slower rendering on big documents.

PNG or JPG — which is better for documents?

PNG for text-heavy pages: edges stay perfectly sharp and compression is lossless. JPG for photographic pages: much smaller files with no visible loss at the quality used here.

Can I convert only some pages?

Yes. Every rendered page has its own download button, so you can take page 3 alone or use 'Download all pages' for the complete set. Each page arrives as a separate image file.

Why won't my PDF open?

Password-protected PDFs cannot be rendered until the owner removes protection. A small number of damaged or extremely large files may also exceed browser memory — try closing other tabs and reloading.

Is there a page or file-size limit?

There is no imposed limit and no watermark. Practical limits are your device's memory: very long documents at 300 DPI render more slowly, so step down to 200 DPI if a huge file struggles.
